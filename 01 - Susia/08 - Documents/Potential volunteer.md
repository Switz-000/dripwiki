Potential volunteer

Today, the sun decided to scrape my scales. It has made my limbs numb, my back a bright red and my eyes swollen. It stares at me so harshly I do not dare stare back at it. Worst of all, my socks are wet.

There is sand all over my face, sand in every pocket, in every hole; it even crawls under my nails, under my tongue and under each and every one of my thoughts.

If men and fish were made to be separate, what terrible crime must I have committed to become like one? I could swim the world as I am, but I still hear duty calling inland. Something tells me to lift my fins from the sand. 

Instead of moving, I check my left pocket. An envelope. It has no destination; every field is empty. To take a fish from the sea is one arduous step; to deliver a message to one is, arguably, harder.

Inside, a paper, folded exactly six times. It is obvious that the man who printed this could not even fold a seventh time. He lacks any authority, unable to impose his will upon this paper.

```

This message was generated automatically. Please do not reply.
```

I am in complete awe. The republic itself has drafted a letter for me. What could I have done to be officially called by the state to stand before a trial? How can they summon me without my knowing the subject of these proceedings?

And they ask me to dial? How am I supposed to defend myself over the phone or through the internet? Perhaps I judged the man who folded this too harshly. No. I am at war with him, and soon it is his turn to judge me.

I get up. My shoes are uphill, safely above the tide line, where I must have left them. I put them on over my wet socks.

By the shore, I see a little man, no older than twelve, digging a small hole. His knees are pressed against the scalding sand; he is clearly determined to achieve a futile and ultimately doomed goal.

I approach him and say: "Toil, toil, little landman! For this world is always short of sea! It is in your sweat, it is in your tears! You were born inland, but do not forget: everything the sea creates, it takes back!"

He looks at me unglamorously, dissecting me with his eyes, and replies: "Are you the delivery man? My dad is waiting for his beer."

I reply: "What? Do you take me for a delivery man? I have lectured you on the problems of the real and the metaphysical, and you scrub me off with such meaningless indifference? The tide will come, and it will destroy everything you have built. Do you not understand?"

The boy just looks at me and says: "I do. I like digging holes."

I turn back and make a strategic retreat. I keep walking by the shore, reading the letter over and over again. The scalding heat is baking my head, yet I seem closer and closer to figuring out the message.

>     You have been chosen from a random selection of citizens to serve as a potential volunteer
>     You have fifteen days to access the official website at ttj.orlitia.su
>     You should follow the instructions on the site
>     You should answer the questions honestly

I see across the street a bench in the shade. Tempting. A gust takes the paper from my hand and carries it over the water. I do not chase it.

While crossing the street, I close my eyes to retain it better and repeat what I have gathered from it.

>     I was chosen from a random selection of citizens
>     Some citizens serve as volunteers to the state
>     I have 15 days to answer the questions honestly
>     I need to follow the instructions on the site

As I finish repeating my mission, I hear the violent screech of a whale. I turn, and some sort of white land vessel has stopped in front of me. How have I not heard this three-ton machine sailing towards me? I knock on the captain's window. He does not reply. I squint through the black-tinted glass, but I cannot see anyone inside the tight cabin.

Before I can act at all, the white vessel accelerates toward the horizon. Is the state developing three-ton silent death machines? What else could they be hiding?

As I sit on the bench, I finally have a break from the wicked fireball in the sky. Now I can think about what I should do next.

I do not reply. I expect him to fill the peace with more violent ramblings. He, instead, flicks his cigarette over the railing. I watch it fall the same ten stories, until the river carries it towards my home. He does not say goodbye.
