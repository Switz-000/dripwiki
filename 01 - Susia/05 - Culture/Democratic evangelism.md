**Democratic evangelism** is the position in Susian foreign policy thought that Susia bears a moral obligation to actively spread democracy and liberal values beyond its borders. It encompasses support for pro-democracy movements in foreign nations, covert and overt regime change operations, and the conditioning of foreign aid and trade agreements on democratic reforms. The position is rooted in [[Susian Exceptionalism|Susian exceptionalist]] ideology and the Versijian argument that natural rights are universal and therefore their denial abroad is a wrong that Susia has standing to address. It is distinct from the purely defensive orientation of the [[Fortress complex]], though the two frequently operate together in practice.

## Origins

Democratic evangelism emerged from the same Versijian philosophical tradition that underlies the broader exceptionalist framework: if rights are inalienable and universal, then the existence of governments that deny them is not simply a foreign political matter but a moral condition with implications for Susia. The obligation to act, in this reading, follows from the belief that Susia's model encodes discovered truths about human freedom rather than merely one cultural preference among others.

In its early republican form, this philosophical impulse was largely latent. The [[Tahuni Accords]] republic was a new and fragile state with limited capacity for foreign activism. The founding foreign policy priority of [[Suizo Soites|Soites]] was survival and consolidation, not expansion of the democratic model.

## The Continental Divide and anti-syndicalist interventionism

> *Main article: [[Continental Divide]]*

Democratic evangelism acquired its operational character during the [[Continental Divide]] (1957–1977). The ideological competition with [[Confia]]'s syndicalist government under [[Lorelaj Paulowić]] produced the first systematic Susian program of foreign political intervention. Susia funded opposition movements, ran covert operations against Confian-aligned governments across the continent, and organized the [[ONDD]] in 1961 as the institutional vehicle for its anti-syndicalist alliance.

This phase was not framed primarily in the language of moral obligation. Its justification was strategic: syndicalism spreading across the continent posed an existential threat to the Susian model, and intervention was self-defense as much as principle. The moral and strategic arguments were not distinguished, and they reinforced each other. Stopping syndicalism was both the right thing to do and the necessary thing to do.

The [[Continental War]] (1975–1977) was the most direct expression of this convergence. Susia committed military force to defeat the syndicalist bloc, and the subsequent [[Flower Revolutions]] brought democratic governments to Confia and several other states. The post-war period was the high point of democratic evangelism as a practical foreign policy program: Susian-backed democratic transitions across the continent were framed as vindication of the belief that Susia's model was universally applicable and that intervention on its behalf produced durable results.

## Post-war evolution

The success of the Flower Revolutions entrenched democratic evangelism as a legitimate position in Susian foreign policy debate, with the post-war decade as its reference case. Its proponents argued that active promotion of democracy had transformed the continent from a contested ideological battleground into a zone of aligned states. The [[ONDD]] evolved from an anti-syndicalist instrument into a broader democratic alliance, and Susian foreign aid increasingly carried democratic reform conditions.

The Confian case also illustrated democratic evangelism's most favorable possible outcome: a former adversary integrated into a permanent partnership through the ONDD itself (1993)  and eventually into the [[Lasman Economic Initiative]] (2000). Proponents of democratic evangelism treat the Confian trajectory as the model for what the position, pursued consistently, can achieve.

Critics noted even in this period that the Flower Revolutions had been produced by military defeat followed by Susian political pressure, and that the durable nature of the resulting democracies owed as much to Confia's own internal conditions as to Susian intervention. The question of whether democracy can be reliably exported, or whether the Confian case was exceptional, has never been resolved in Susian foreign policy thought.

## The Ashgerad era and contemporary form

The emergence of [[Ashgerad]] as the primary adversary after 2006 reshaped democratic evangelism without dissolving it. The [[War of Arempur]] (2012) demonstrated that Susia could not simply project its model outward through military or political pressure against a peer civilizational rival. Ashgerad was neither a syndicalist state susceptible to the same pressure that had worked against Confia, nor an internally unstable government vulnerable to pro-democracy movements. The civilizational incompatibility was more fundamental.

Contemporary democratic evangelism operates primarily through softer instruments: funding pro-democracy civil society organizations in non-aligned states, tying trade and aid agreements to governance conditions, and maintaining the ONDD as a standing demonstration that the democratic model produces stable and prosperous alliances. The Frumentarze's [[Foreign Operations Division]] runs covert support programs for democratic movements in states where Ashgeradn influence is growing, though the scale and specifics of these operations remain classified.

The position retains political support across most of the Susian spectrum, though its emphasis varies. Interventionist factions within the [[Democratic-Federalist Party]] and elements of the [[Modernist Alliance]] treat it as a strategic imperative tied directly to the Ashgerad competition: a world with more democracies is a world where Susia has more allies and where the Ashgeradn model has less room to expand. More cautious voices within all parties argue that the gap between the democratic evangelism's stated ambitions and its actual record outside the post-war Confian context is wide enough to warrant restraint.

## Tension with the Fortress Complex

Democratic evangelism and the [[Fortress complex]] are not identical and are not always compatible. The Fortress Complex orients Susian policy toward defense of what exists. Democratic evangelism orients it toward expansion of the model into territory it does not yet hold. In practice, the two have usually coexisted because the Confian case made both positions look like the same policy: defending the republic required defeating syndicalism, and defeating syndicalism required democratic transitions in formerly hostile states. The Ashgerad cold war makes the tension more visible, because Ashgerad cannot be defeated in the way Confia was, and expanding the democratic model into Ashgeradn spheres of influence carries risks that the earlier interventionist tradition did not face.
