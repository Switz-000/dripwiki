A **Free Economic Zone** (FEZ) is a city-scale territory within [[Susia]] leased by the federal government to a [[Yarnojte]], a corporation granted strategic status by the [[Secretariat of Efficiency]], which governs the zone in place of a conventional municipal or state authority. FEZs were created by the [[Susian Constitution|constitution of 2038]] as part of the [[The Great Transition|Great Transition]] and are the structural core of the [[Susia|Techno-Federative Republic]]'s distinctive governing model. Within their boundaries, the governing Yarnojte provides housing, healthcare, education, infrastructure, and internal law enforcement. Residents retain full federal constitutional protections. Participation is voluntary: no Susian is required to live or work in a FEZ.

As of 2080, approximately 0.25% of the Susian population lives inside a FEZ. [[Yarnojte|Yarnojtes]] and their subsidiaries employ roughly 3% of the total Susian workforce, the majority of whom live and work in independent Susia rather than inside the zones themselves.

## Origins

> *Main article: [[The Great Transition]] , [[Kolkovianism]]

The FEZ concept has a contested intellectual genealogy. [[Yário Kolkov]], writing in the 1980s and 1990s, articulated a theory of competitive institutional pluralism in which corporations, traditional democratic governments, and other governance forms would coexist, compete for residents, and adapt or lose population. His framework treated institutional competition as the mechanism by which democracy could evolve rather than calcify. Kolkov never specified a blueprint for corporate governance zones, and he was explicit that competition, federal oversight, and easy exit were conditions without which his framework would collapse into corporate capture rather than institutional evolution.

The [[Modernist Alliance]], which drove the constitutional convention of 2036 to 2038, formed around Kolkov's ideas but was not ideologically unified. Corporate modernists within the movement, backed by substantial lobbying from [[Troli Ustaras]] and [[Nayotai]], pressed for an interpretation of Kolkov's work that centered corporate FEZ governance as the primary form of institutional competition. The resulting constitutional text reflected this faction's influence. Kolkov himself, who died in 2011, had already identified that TU was stripping his framework of the oversight and exit provisions he considered essential.

The FEZ system as ratified narrowed Kolkov's pluralist vision into a specifically corporate model. Whether it constitutes a valid implementation of competitive governance principles or a corporate co-optation of a philosophy that never endorsed it remains a genuine line of debate in Susian political theory.

## Creation process

FEZs are created through a multi-stage process involving the host state, the [[Secretariat of Efficiency]], and the public.

A state committee first delimits a zone within state territory. The state legislature and governor must approve the delimitation before it proceeds. The zone then passes to federal administrative control, remaining under state jurisdiction, and the Secretariat opens a competitive bidding process. Yarnojtes submit bids and governance proposals; the host state negotiates with candidates while the Secretariat participates as mediator representing federal and long-term planning interests.

Once a Yarnojte is selected and a draft lease is prepared, the Secretariat commissions an independent analysis of the FEZ's projected long-term effects and costs. This analysis is published as a plain-language public document of no more than ten pages. A public referendum is then held in the affected area, requiring between three-quarters to four-fifths approval to pass, depending on the terms of the specific proposal.

If the referendum passes, the lease is signed by the state, the Yarnojte, and the Secretariat. Leases typically run for thirty years and may be renegotiated at any time by agreement of all three parties. At expiration, a renewal referendum requires two-thirds approval. If the Yarnojte violates the lease terms, breaches federal constitutional minimums, or loses Yarnojte status, the lease is voided immediately and a referendum is held to determine whether the territory reverts to state jurisdiction or is re-offered through the bidding process.

FEZs are scarce by design with only a maximum of 12 being allowed constitutionally. The creation process is demanding, states typically have negotiating leverage over Yarnojtes because the zones are difficult to obtain, and the Secretariat is institutionally resistant to approving zones it does not judge to be in the long-term national interest.

## Governance structure

> *Main articles: [[Yarnojte]], [[Secretariat of Efficiency]]*

Inside a FEZ, the governing Yarnojte holds broad internal autonomy. It sets internal regulations, manages the built environment, provides services, and operates its own internal security. The corporate culture of the Yarnojte shapes the zone's character in practice: living in a [[Troli Ustaras]] FEZ is a substantially different experience from living in a [[Soites Group]] or [[Atompron]] zone.

Federal constitutional minimums apply regardless of the Yarnojte's governance preferences. Residents retain freedom of speech, freedom of movement, the right to leave the zone at any time, and all other rights enumerated in the constitution. The Secretariat enforces these minimums and retains authority to suspend a FEZ's autonomy in a crisis or void the lease for non-compliance. The [[Federal Authority of Commerce and Competition]] (AFCC) retains jurisdiction over commerce law within FEZs.

States retain formal jurisdictional authority over FEZ territory. Certain state-level regulations apply inside the zones, though the degree of state involvement varies by the terms of individual leases.

## Right to exit

The right to leave a FEZ at any time, without penalty, is constitutionally protected and treated in Susian legal doctrine as the mechanism that makes voluntary participation meaningful. The dominant Modernist argument for the FEZ system rests on this provision: entry into a FEZ constitutes informed consent to that Yarnojte's governance model, and continued residence constitutes ongoing consent, precisely because departure is always available.

Critics argue that this framing obscures the economic coercion involved. For workers whose skills are specifically sought by a single Yarnojte, or whose housing and healthcare are provided through the FEZ, the practical cost of exit is high enough that "voluntary" participation describes the legal structure rather than the lived experience. This critique is most frequently applied to the higher-clearance FEZs operated by [[Van Ritter]], where workers at the top security tiers face travel restrictions and family surveillance that make exit formally available but practically constrained.

## Fiscal role

FEZ taxes are paid at standard federal and state rates. In states with large FEZ presences, this tax revenue is significant: [[Postia]] and [[Dripia]] in particular depend substantially on Yarnojte tax contributions to fund state-level universal healthcare and education services in independent Susia. Susians living in states without FEZs benefit indirectly through the consumer prices made possible by Yarnojte-scale production, as well as through federal services partially funded by Yarnojte taxation.

Yarnojtes pay FEZ lease fees in [[Federal Reserve Unit|Federal Drips]] (FD$), the commodity-backed currency used for long-term contracts, which provides the host state and federal government with inflation-stable long-term revenue.

## Scale and public perception

The FEZ population is small relative to Susia as a whole. The zones are prominent in political discourse and cultural life substantially out of proportion to the number of people who live in them. For residents of the central states, contact with FEZ life is common: a significant share of Susians in [[Postia]], [[Dripia]], [[Sužielaj]], and [[Misocévia]] have worked in a FEZ at some point or have family members who have. In the southern states, particularly [[Orlítia]] and [[Vitrika]], the zones are more distant from daily experience, and both states have developed political cultures defined in part by their explicit rejection of the FEZ model.

The [[Coalition of State's Rights]] treats the FEZ system with consistent skepticism, viewing concentrated corporate authority over territory as incompatible with both state sovereignty and individual freedom. The [[Democratic-Federalist Party]] accepts the system while pressing for stronger labor protections and broader constitutional minimums within the zones. The [[Modernist Alliance]] treats the FEZ system as a foundational achievement of the Great Transition, pointing to economic productivity, infrastructure development, and technological advancement as validation of the model.

At the cultural level, FEZs are a recurring subject of Susian popular media, treated alternately as aspirational, dystopian, or simply ordinary depending on the source. The saying "It's misocévian good" (mediocre) contrasts implicitly with FEZ standards; the slang term [[Ňotrídulij]] (lit. "servant of capital"), used to describe white-collar Yarnojte workers characterized by exceptional loyalty and long hours, reflects a degree of cultural ambivalence about what FEZ employment produces in the people who do it.
