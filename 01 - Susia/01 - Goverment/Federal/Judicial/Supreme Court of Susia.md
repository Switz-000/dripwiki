The **Susian Supreme Court** is the highest court of [[Susia]] and the final interpreter of the [[Susian Constitution]]. Established with the republic in 1954, it is composed of eleven justices who serve for life and sit together on every case. It hears few cases, confines itself to fundamental constitutional questions, and issues its decisions as a single unsigned opinion rather than under the names of individual justices.

## History

### Imperial origins

> *Main article: [[Dripstanian Wars of Religion]]*

The Court descends from the body of eleven justices established under [[Emperor Agamilos]] in the religious settlement of the Dripstanian Wars of Religion, to rule on the official interpretation of the Holy [[Ladan]] for the church Agamilos had united under the crown. Its members were drawn from the scholars of the empire's first three universities, which competed to place them on it. Because imperial law was justified by scripture, the body's authority over interpretation grew during the imperial era into the role of the empire's highest court. It kept its theological mandate until the dissolution of the empire.

### Establishment

The Supreme Court was established in 1954 as part of the constitutional settlement that founded the republic. The [[Susian Constitution]] took the place of the Ladan as the text the body interprets, and the body kept its form: eleven justices serving for life, all sitting on every case, a single unsigned opinion in the name of the whole, and sealed deliberations. The justification for keeping a court above ordinary majorities came from the constitutional theory of [[Armadesh Versij]], whose work held that a body of enumerated rights placed beyond the reach of ordinary majorities required an independent judiciary empowered to strike down laws that violated them. The Court was authorized directly by the constitution rather than by statute, placing its existence and core powers beyond the amendment of any single government.

The Court's foundational role was established early through its ruling on the constitutionality of party paramilitary organizations during the [[Postia Crisis]], which applied the constitution's protections to the question of armed political association and set the terms for federal enforcement that followed.

## Structure

The Court is composed of eleven justices who serve for life. Justices are nominated by the [[President of Susia|president]] and confirmed by a supermajority of the [[Susian Senate]]. There is no fixed retirement age, and a seat becomes vacant only on the death or resignation of its holder.

All eleven justices sit on every case; the Court does not divide into panels. Because it hears only questions of fundamental constitutional interpretation, its caseload is small, and most legal disputes in Susia are resolved by state courts without ever reaching it.

The Court issues each decision as a single opinion attributed to the Court as a whole. Individual justices are not named as authors, and the Court does not publish vote counts, concurrences, or dissents. The internal deliberations and the division of votes behind a ruling are sealed for 20 years, after which the full record is opened.

## Controversies

### Kolkov's critique

[[Yário Kolkov]] criticized the Court in [[Food for the Fools]] (1994) and returned to the argument at greater length in [[Evolution of Governance]] (1997). His target was less Versij's philosophy than the treatment of Versij: the republic, he argued, treated Versij as a saint, as the empire had treated its dead emperors, and the Court was his principal example. In his account the Court did as much for the governance of the republic as the eleven-member tribunal had done for the empire, which had not survived. The problem of governance, he argued, was not bad ideas but the inability to exchange bad ideas for good ones, and he read the previous five centuries of history as the record of that failure. In his formulation the constitution was the republic's Holy Ladan. Versijians have generally received the comparison as a compliment.
