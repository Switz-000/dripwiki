The **National Care Act of 1981** is a [[Susia|Susian]] [[Susian Federal Government|federal]] law that established a universal health insurance baseline available to all residents of the republic. Sponsored by Senator [[Bejňar Garev]] of [[Troli]] and enacted during the presidency of [[Čalsor Boňár]], it provides federally funded coverage for a defined standard of care. The constitution does not enumerate a federal power over healthcare, and the Act rests on the federal taxation and spending power.

## Background

Federal activity in public health preceded the Act. During the post-war reconstruction the federal government undertook programs in communicable disease control and preventive medicine, fields in which provision organized below the national level was ineffective because transmission does not stop at a state border. The administrative capacity built through those programs was in place before 1981.

The Act was part of the expansion of federal authority undertaken during the post-war reconstruction under [[Čalsor Boňár]], the same program of state-directed development that established the state energy company [[Atompron]]. It extended federal activity into a field the constitution does not assign to the federal government.

As a spending measure, the bill originated in the [[Susian Senate]] as required by the constitution. It was introduced by Garev, a senator from Troli, the president's home state.

## Provisions

The Act established a federally funded insurance program open to all residents. The program is a financing mechanism rather than a provider of care: delivery remains with state health systems and private providers, while the federal program pays for covered services.

Coverage extends to the established standard of care, comprising primary and preventive care, emergency and acute hospital treatment, maternity and childbirth, a formulary of essential medicines, standard management of common chronic conditions, basic mental health care, and routine diagnostics. Treatment outside the established standard is not covered, including rare-disease treatment, experimental and frontier therapies, life extension, enhancement medicine, and elective or premium services.


## Effects

The Act set a national minimum rather than a uniform national system. States retained the power to provide healthcare beyond the federal baseline, and provision came to track state fiscal capacity. Wealthier states such as [[Dripia]] built comprehensive public systems above the baseline, while poorer states such as [[Nessel]] and [[Vitrika]] relied on the baseline as the whole of their public provision.

Care falling outside the covered standard, including rare-disease treatment and life extension, is obtained through private providers, among them [[Lischev-Verene]]. Residents of [[Free Economic Zone|Free Economic Zones]] retain federal coverage in addition to the services provided by the governing [[Yarnojte]].

## Controversy

The Act was among the contested elements of Boňár's expansion of federal power, which his critics held to be a departure from the limited-government design of the constitution. Opponents argued that the constitution grants the federal government no authority over healthcare, and that the Act created a permanent federal function out of an appropriation. Opposition was concentrated in the [[Susian Liberal Party]] and among figures associated with [[Filňar Čantij]]. The Act was defended as an exercise of the federal taxation and spending power rather than as an assumption of any service obligation.

Because the program rests on the spending power rather than on a constitutional obligation, it is subject to amendment or repeal by ordinary legislation. The scope of the covered standard of care is set by statute and has remained a subject of legislative dispute.
