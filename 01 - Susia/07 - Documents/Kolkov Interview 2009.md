TRANSCRIPT BEGINS

*Kolkov is already smoking when I arrive. The apartment is small, cramped, books stacked on every surface, papers scattered across a table that looks like it came from a salvage yard, ashtray overflowing with [[Vitrika|vitrikan]] cigarette butts. Through the window, I can see the [[Nayotai]] factory complex a few blocks away. The walls are bare except for a faded poster from a union rally.*

**RAROSKA:** Thank you for agreeing to meet, Mr. Kolkov. I know you don't do many interviews.

**KOLKOV:** I don't do any interviews. I made an exception because you said this was for academic purposes, not corporate propaganda. Was that true, or did [[Troli Ustaras]] sponsor your department too?

**RAROSKA:** No... I mean, we receive no corporate funding. [[University of New Kentu]] has strict policies-

**KOLKOV:** Good. Keep it that way. The minute TU money touches your budget, you become theirs. They're very patient about it. Very systematic. So what do you want to know?

**RAROSKA:** Your work is generating significant interest, both in academic circles and in the private sector. "The Evolution of Governance" is now assigned reading in-

**KOLKOV:** Three departments at your university. I know. You said that in your email. You also said TU has been "reaching out." That's a polite way of putting it.

**RAROSKA:** They've named several initiatives after you, I understand. The [[Kolkov Institute for Innovation]], the [[Kolkov Award for Engineering]]-

**KOLKOV:** Do you know what they're doing? Do you actually understand what's happening here?

**RAROSKA:** I... assume they're honoring your intellectual contributions-

**KOLKOV:** Honoring. For fuck's sake. They're not honoring me, Professor. They're necrophiliacs. They're fucking my corpse before I'm even dead.

**RAROSKA:** That's... graphic.

**KOLKOV:** It's accurate. I worked at TU for two years. 1987 to 1989. Worst two years of my life, and I spent five years at Nayotai, so that's saying something. You want to know why I left TU? Because I watched them turn work into religion. I watched brilliant engineers convinced they were saving humanity by building better computers. I watched people cry, actually cry, when they talked about the company mission.

**RAROSKA:** You wrote about this in "[[Letters to a Dead Republic]]"-

**KOLKOV:** I wrote that TU makes believers out of people, and believers are more dangerous than slaves. At least slaves know they're in chains. Now those same believers, the people who represent everything I warned against, are naming buildings after me. They're putting my face on their walls. They're turning me into a patron saint of the exact missionary zealotry I spent twenty years criticizing.

**RAROSKA:** But surely they're responding to your ideas about competitive systems, about corporate-

**KOLKOV:** They're responding to six-word soundbites ripped from context. Let me show you something. (rummages through papers, pulls out a glossy brochure) This arrived last week. "The Kolkov Institute for Innovation, where tomorrow's pioneers carry forward the legacy of visionary thinking." You see this quote they used? "Evolution has no endpoint." They put it next to a picture of their shiny new robotics lab. The message is clear: we're evolution in action, we're the future, resistance is pointless. They took my argument against teleological thinking and turned it into a justification for exactly that.

**RAROSKA:** That's... quite a reinterpretation.

**KOLKOV:** It's not a reinterpretation. It's intellectual vandalism. Every single quote they use-every single one-means the opposite of what I wrote. "Adapt or die"? I was talking about democratic systems needing flexibility. They printed it on coffee mugs. Coffee mugs, Professor. As a threat to anyone who doesn't conform to their corporate culture.

**RAROSKA:** Have you considered asking them to stop?

**KOLKOV:** (stops pacing, stares at her) Asking them? Asking them? These are people who think they're prophets of technological salvation. These are people who have HR departments that use cult deprogramming techniques in reverse. These are people who convinced their employees that a 100-hour work week is liberation because they're "building tomorrow."

**RAROSKA:** The work week at TU is actually-

**KOLKOV:** (interrupting) I don't care what the official policy says. I know how TU works. The official policy is one thing. The culture is another. And the culture says: if you're not working constantly, you don't believe hard enough. If you don't believe hard enough, you're not really TU. And now they want to make me their intellectual godfather.

**RAROSKA:** Why do you think they're doing this?

**KOLKOV:** Because I'm becoming influential. "[[Evolution of Governance]]" is being taught in universities. People in government are citing my work. There's talk-just talk, but still-of corporate governance zones, federalist experimentation, adaptive institutions. And TU sees an opportunity.

**RAROSKA:** An opportunity for what?

**KOLKOV:** To capture the narrative before anyone else does. To make sure that when people think about corporate zones competing with democratic government, they think: "Oh, like TU. Like Kolkov's old employer. Like the Kolkov Institute." They're laundering their reputation through my name. Making themselves synonymous with my ideas before my ideas even get implemented.

**RAROSKA:** But surely they're taking a risk. If your ideas become policy and fail-

**KOLKOV:** They're not worried about failure. They're worried about being left out. TU has been lobbying for lighter regulation for years. They want to run their own company towns. They want autonomy from state governments. My work-my theoretical framework-gives them intellectual cover for what they wanted to do anyway.

**RAROSKA:** So your theory legitimizes their agenda?

**KOLKOV:** (annoyed) No, no. My theory says: corporations are powerful, they're not going away, so build systems where they compete with each other instead of capturing the government. Multiple corporations, multiple models, genuine competition, easy exit, strong oversight. That's the theory.

**RAROSKA:** And TU-

**KOLKOV:** TU hears: "Let us run things." They ignore the competition part. They ignore the oversight part. They ignore the exit part. They just hear: "Corporate governance is legitimate" and they run with it. And now I'm their philosopher-king, apparently.

**RAROSKA:** The irony is-

**KOLKOV:** The irony is that I spent an entire chapter warning against exactly this. Philosophical capture, I called it. When corporations don't just lobby for favorable policy, but capture the intellectual frameworks that generate policy.

**RAROSKA:** And now you're that thinker.

**KOLKOV:** And I never took a fucking dime from them. That's the beautiful part. They don't need to buy me. They just need to selectively quote me, and voila-instant legitimacy.

**RAROSKA:** What about the Kolkov Award? That's significant recognition-

**KOLKOV:** (interrupting angrily) Recognition? You think I want their fucking recognition? You think I want to be associated with a company where people have identity crises when they quit because they've forgotten who they are without the mission? Let me tell you what the Kolkov Award is. It's not about celebrating good engineering. It's about making sure that every ambitious engineer at TU, every kid who dreams of making a difference associates my name with success at TU. It's grooming. Intellectual grooming.

**RAROSKA:** That seems-

**KOLKOV:** Extreme? Is it? (picks up another brochure) Look at this. The ceremony next month. "Celebrating the legacy of Yário Kolkov, whose two years at TU helped shape his revolutionary thinking about adaptive systems and corporate innovation." Two years. I was there for two years, and I left because I was disgusted. But according to this, those two years were "formative."

**RAROSKA:** Were they?

**KOLKOV:** Yes. They formed my opposition to everything TU represents. They taught me that corporate mission is a cult by another name. They taught me that believers are more dangerous than cynics because at least cynics know they're full of shit. (lights another cigarette) But TU doesn't want that story. They want the story where young Kolkov learned valuable lessons at TU that led to his brilliant theories. It's origin-story bullshit.

**RAROSKA:** You could refuse to attend-

**KOLKOV:** I did refuse. They're having the ceremony anyway. My absence will be explained as "scheduling conflicts" or "Professor Kolkov's well-known preference for privacy." They'll unveil the award. They'll talk about my legacy. They'll quote my work out of context. And I'll be at home, smoking cigarettes and hating every minute of it.

**RAROSKA:** Why not publicly repudiate them?

**KOLKOV:** (long pause, smoking) Because that makes them more important, not less. If I denounce TU, I become "the philosopher who denounced TU." Every article about me starts with the controversy. Every interview becomes about them. And besides, it won't stop them. They'll just pivot to "even those who challenge us engage with our ideas" or some corporate communications horseshit.

**RAROSKA:** So you do nothing?

**KOLKOV:** I don't do nothing. I write. I keep writing. I make the arguments they don't want to hear. I add complexity they can't simplify. I stay alive and inconvenient. And I refuse to be their prophet.

**RAROSKA:** But they're making you one anyway.

**KOLKOV:** Yes. Because that's what believers do. They don't follow prophet, they make them. They need prophets to legitimate their faith. And if the prophet says "I'm not your prophet," they just say "look how humble he is."

**RAROSKA:** That's rather cynical.

**KOLKOV:** It's observant. You know what I wrote about idol-worship? Three years ago, in "[[The Tyranny of Order]]"? I wrote: "Beware anyone who turns thinkers into icons, for icons don't argue back. Beware anyone who takes living philosophy and makes it dead doctrine. And beware especially the organizations that need prophets more than they need truth." Guess which organization was i talking about?

**RAROSKA:** TU.

**KOLKOV:** TU. Who are currently building a "Kolkov Learning Center" where young engineers will be inspired by carefully curated quotes from a man who thinks their entire corporate culture is pathological. The students will walk past "Evolution has no endpoint" on their way to their mandatory team-building exercises. They'll read "Adaptation is survival" right before a motivational speech about TU's five-year plan. And they'll think: this Kolkov guy gets it. He understands us.

**RAROSKA:** And they'll never read the full context.

**KOLKOV:** Why would they? The full context is uncomfortable. The full context challenges their worldview. The soundbites confirm it. (taps ash) That's how intellectual capture works, Professor. You don't need to silence critics. You just need to make criticism look like endorsement from the right angle.

**RAROSKA:** What do you say to TU employees who genuinely admire your work? Who see you as an inspiration?

**KOLKOV:** (long exhale of smoke) I'd say: read the whole fucking book. Not the quotes on your office wall. Not the summaries in your corporate newsletter. The actual book. All of it. Including the uncomfortable parts. Especially the uncomfortable parts.

**RAROSKA:** And if they do?

**KOLKOV:** Then they'll have a choice. They can keep believing in the mission, keep telling themselves they're building tomorrow, keep optimizing their lives toward corporate goals. Or they can wake up and realize they're in a cage that's beautiful and comfortable and utterly totalizing. The cog knows it's being used. The believer thinks exploitation is enlightenment.

**RAROSKA:** You wrote that about your time at TU.

**KOLKOV:** And it's still true. More true now than it was then. TU has gotten better at making believers. Better at the psychology, better at the culture, better at making people forget there are alternatives. (looks out window) And now they want to use my name to make even more believers.

**RAROSKA:** Can I ask about your current work?

**KOLKOV:** I'm writing about [[competitive federalism]]. How to structure systems where different governance models compete without one dominating. Corporate zones, traditional states, experimental communities-all coexisting, all competing for residents, all forced to improve because people can leave.

**RAROSKA:** That sounds like it would appeal to TU.

**KOLKOV:** Of course it appeals to TU. They hear "corporate zones" and think: we can run our own city-states. They ignore the "competing" part. They ignore the "people can leave" part. They just hear: autonomy. Power. No oversight.

**RAROSKA:** Is that not what you're arguing for?

**KOLKOV:** I'm arguing for competitive pressure. I'm arguing for escape valves. I'm arguing that if we're going to have corporate power-and we are, whether we like it or not-then we better make damn sure corporations have to compete with each other AND with traditional democratic governance. Multiple options. Easy exit. Real choice.

**RAROSKA:** And TU-

**KOLKOV:** TU wants a monopoly with extra steps. They want to be the only option so compelling that leaving becomes unthinkable. They don't want to compete-they want to win so thoroughly that competition becomes irrelevant. (exhales smoke) And they want to use my theory to justify it.

**RAROSKA:** What happens if your ideas get implemented and it goes wrong?

**KOLKOV:** Then I'll be blamed for enabling corporate tyranny. The critics will say: "See? Kolkov's theories just gave corporations what they always wanted." And they'll be right, in a way. Not because my theories are wrong, but because the implementation ignored all the safeguards.

**RAROSKA:** And if it goes right?

**KOLKOV:** (bitter smile) Then TU will take all the credit. "The Kolkov Institute's visionary leadership helped build this system." They'll write themselves into the story. They'll make sure everyone knows that Kolkov-their Kolkov, the TU alumnus-inspired this new world. Either way, they win.

**RAROSKA:** That's rather bleak.

**KOLKOV:** Bleak is my baseline. But I keep writing because maybe.... MAYBE someone will read past the soundbites. Maybe someone will see what TU is doing and recognize it for what it is. Maybe someone will build the system right instead of building TU's fantasy of it.

**RAROSKA:** Do you think that's likely?

**KOLKOV:** No. But evolution isn't about likely outcomes. It's about possible ones. I'm making it possible for someone, someday, to build this right. That's all I can do. The rest is out of my hands. Ideas evolve, mutate, get coopted. That's their nature.

**RAROSKA:** Even if TU corrupts it?

**KOLKOV:** Especially if TU corrupts it. Because someday, someone will compare TU's version to mine. They'll see what got lost in translation. They'll see what was stripped out, what was emphasized, what was inverted. And maybe they'll understand that corporate power needs constraints, not celebration.

**RAROSKA:** You're very committed to this.

**KOLKOV:** I watched people at TU cry about quarterly targets. I watched grown ass engineers have existential crises because they weren't "contributing enough to the mission." I watched brilliant people turn themselves into company assets and call it self-actualization. If my work helps build a world where that becomes universal? Where every workplace is TU? I'll have failed in the worst possible way.

**RAROSKA:** Then why write about corporate governance at all?

**KOLKOV:** Because corporate power exists whether I write about it or not. The question isn't whether corporations have power-they do. The question is: do we structure that power intelligently, or do we let it metastasize unchecked? I'm trying to build a cage for the beast. TU thinks I'm building them a throne.

**RAROSKA:** (closing notebook) Thank you for your time, Mr. Kolkov. This has been... illuminating.

**KOLKOV:** (waves dismissively) Yeah, yeah. Tell your colleagues to keep TU money out of their departments. Tell them to read full books, not abstracts. Tell them to be skeptical of anyone who turns philosophers into prophets. (stands) And tell them that if they see "Kolkov Institute" on anything, it's being used against me, not by me.

**RAROSKA:** I will. 

TRANSCRIPT ENDS

INTERVIEWER'S NOTE:

Six weeks after this interview, Troli Ustaras held a dedication ceremony for the [[Kolkov Learning Center]] at their [[Donetruže]] headquarters. The event was attended by over 800 employees and executives, regional politicians, and several journalists. The main hall features seventeen Kolkov quotations in large typography, including "Evolution has no endpoint," "Adapt or die," "Diversity is life," and "Systems that don't evolve go extinct."

The promotional materials describe Kolkov as "political theorist, engineer, and TU alumnus (1987-1989) whose groundbreaking work on adaptive governance and competitive systems continues to inspire innovators worldwide."

None of the displayed quotes include their surrounding context. Several are taken from passages explicitly criticizing corporate culture. The word "believer" appears nowhere in the learning center, despite being central to Kolkov's critique of TU in "Letters to a Dead Republic."

When I contacted TU's communications department for comment on Kolkov's absence from the ceremony, I received the following response: "Professor Kolkov's preference for privacy is well-known and respected. We remain honored by his intellectual legacy and grateful for his formative years with TU, which helped shape his revolutionary thinking."

I sent Kolkov a copy of this statement. He sent back a postcard with one sentence: "Believers making prophets out of heretics. Guess I called that one."

— Y.R., University of New Kentu, December 2009

EPILOGUE (Added February 2011)

I visited Kolkov last month. His health is deteriorating rapidly, lung cancer, stage four. He continues to smoke. "What's it going to do, kill me?" he said, lighting another cigarette. "Too late for prevention now."

He was working on revisions to "Democracy as Organism," his next book on constitutional evolution and federal experimentation. On his desk, prominently displayed: a coffee mug from TU with "ADAPT OR DIE – KOLKOV INSTITUTE" printed in bold letters.

"They sent me a whole box," he explained. "I use this one for pens. Seems fitting. The most useful thing they've ever done with my work is give me somewhere to put my fucking pens."

I asked if he'd considered writing a public response to TU's appropriation of his work. He shook his head. "It won't matter. When I'm dead, they'll just get louder. They'll host conferences in my name. They'll fund Kolkov Chairs at universities. They'll make sure that when people think about competitive corporate governance, they think 'TU' first and 'Kolkov' second."

"So you've given up?"

"No." He stubbed out his cigarette and lit another. "I'm writing until I can't anymore. Every book, every essay, every lecture makes it harder for them to completely distort what I said. The work is the evidence. Someday, maybe someone will compare their version to mine and see the gap. That's all I can do."

He coughed, a deep, rattling cough that lasted nearly a minute. When he recovered, he was smiling grimly.

"You know what they'll do when I die? They'll commission a biography. Sympathetic academic, TU funding, access to 'exclusive materials.' And that biography will be the definitive story: Kolkov the visionary, whose time at TU opened his eyes to the future of governance. His critiques will be 'youthful idealism.' His warnings will be 'productive skepticism.' His hatred will be edited into ambivalence."

"Can anyone stop them?"

"No." He looked at the TU coffee mug, full of pens. "The corpse can't argue. That's why they're so patient. They don't need to convert me. They just need to outlive me."

We sat in silence for a while, smoke filling the small apartment.

"The believers always win in the end," he said finally. "Not because they're right. Because they're tireless. Because they need to be right. Because doubt would destroy them." Another coughing fit. "I'm just trying to leave enough doubt behind that someday, someone might see through the mythology."

That was three weeks ago. His doctor says he has months, maybe weeks. He's still writing. Still smoking. Still using that goddamn TU coffee mug.

I think he finds the irony sustaining.

— Y.R., February 2011
