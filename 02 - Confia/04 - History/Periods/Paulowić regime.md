From the [[Enabling Amendment]] in Vartelian 21st, 1958 to the [[Bayonet Revolution]] in Verenian 2nd, 1977, Confia was under a totalitarian dictatorship led by [[Lorelaj Paulowić]].

# History

# Politics

The Paulowić government was marked by severe political repression in all spheres of public life. 

De jure, the government structure was reasonably democratic, with each syndicate electing its local leaders, who then elected their state and [[National Assembly of Confian Syndicates|national representatives]]. However, elections within the syndicates were dominated by the ragged-sleeve elite, who always had their candidates victorious. After the [[Enabling Amendment]], the [[Union of Confian Syndicalists]] was the only legal party, with all other organizations banned and persecuted, most notably the [[Progressive Republican Party]].
These elections were riddled with electoral fraud and intimidation, with non-ragged-sleeve candidates having their lives and those of their families threatened by the state apparatus. Votes were not secret, and ballot-box intimidation was widespread. Thus, the ragged-sleeves oversaw an ongoing concentration of their power, particularly in low-income industrial districts such as [[Saint Eriogala]], forming electoral strongholds that persisted even after the fall of the Syndicalist government.
### Political repression

In 1962, the Department of Security of Expression was created, a censorship body that reviewed all content published by independent media. As this measure quickly became bureaucratic and unnecessary, in 1964 the government passed measures banning all forms of publication and public transmission of information not produced by the state or the party. Individuals accused of conspiring against the syndicalist order were arrested, often brutally tortured and killed, their bodies thrown into mass graves.

The labour camp known as the [[Talfaslaž]], a symbol of the General Government's repression in Confia, initially had all its prisoners released in 1956, but they were quickly replaced by captured members of the KWD and later by regime opponents. The People's Security Department transformed the former penal labor prison into a concentration camp, adding new forced labor facilities and torture chambers to keep the prisoners in line. Many government projects used forced labor from these prisoners to reduce construction costs. Rapes, mutilations, and random executions were carried out by the guards. It is estimated that more than 53,000 individuals were detained at the facility, 13,400 of whom have been confirmed dead.

# Economy

## The two main models

#### Orthodox Syndicalism in practice (1956-1960)

Initially, the Paulowić government had granted complete autonomy to the syndicates, with a rigid policy of horizontal organization and democracy at work, with frequent referendums and elections for workplace managers. Thus, each syndicate would function in a decentralized manner, maximizing the freedom that had already been guaranteed to workers with the [[Charter of Labour]]. This policy ended in long-term inefficiency, resulting in economic stagnation in sectors vital to the economy such as coal and iron mining and heavy industry, the latter recovering rapidly after the post-Dripstan shock. The collectivization of fields into agrarian communes caused a small increase in productivity due to the reorganization of unproductive lands, but it did not meet expectations. The period of [[Orthodox Syndicalism]] garnered a total negative GDP growth of 3.4% over four years.

#### Mixed Planning (1960-1977)

Seeing the widespread failure of this policy, Paulowić and the Commissars formulated Mixed Planning, explained in simple language to the people in their own handwritten book, Rethinking Syndicalism (1961). Mixed planning maintained syndicates as an institution of popular power in government, but rejected their absolute control over the economy, establishing state-run and centrally planned conglomerates for the organization, and in many cases direct government control of the economy. These conglomerates were headed by SUPOPLAN, the National Planning Department. The creation of this body allowed for greater government participation in the economy, which chose high-tech manufacturing for its primary investments. There was also the inauguration of major works, such as the President Paulowić Highway, which connected Carlotopolis to Imgospalje, facilitating the flow of production between the cities, and the [[Lasmanože Hydroelectric Power Plant]]. Thus, the Confian economy was finally able to grow, ending a 30-year period of stagnation since the Secession War.

Although the regime's state propaganda aimed to convey the idea that a popular government was reinvesting in its people, the reality was that the highest echelons of SUPOPLAN were dominated by the elite and their cronies, leading to the cartelization and monopolization of the economy by industrial cliques. This economic centralization, while causing the economic growth cycle that followed its implementation, led to cases of embezzlement and accounting fraud that were only exposed to the public after the Revolution of the Bayonets. The steel industry businessman Jagan Kărwenni, a notorious [[Ragged-sleeve|ragged-sleeve]] , multiplied his net worth fourfold during the period he headed the Commissariat of Industry. The largest increase occurred between the 1964 and 1970 centuries, a period during which Kărwenni presided over SUPOPLAN itself.


## Syndicalist urban development

Urban development, particularly in capitals and large cities, was relegated to mayors appointed by Paulowić. In general, housing construction was mixed, with cheaper, more affordable houses being built by the municipal government and more luxurious and expensive houses being built, sold, and rented by so-called housing collectives — local groups that emerged together with the syndicates in the late Empire.
During Mixed Planning, these groups began to dominate the real estate market, leading to the cartelization of construction contracts through geographical division and abusive rents, with little to no competition. Government-built buildings, even with subsidized energy, water, and other costs, were built far from the city centers where economic growth was booming. 
Due to the precariousness or non-existence of public transportation, citizens living in neighborhoods with subsidized buildings were forced to buy cars to get around. These were rarely cheap and high-quality, given the lack of competition resulting from the state monopoly of the automotive industry under [[Žošewoš Machinery]]. Thus, many went into debt at exorbitant interest rates to finance their automobiles, the only means of transportation available.
